Stretch goal · MLCloud or contrail?

Telling a contrail from natural cirrus by its shape and its aircraft

Simulated satellite patches in ash false-colour, where thin ice cloud shows dark blue. Each tile is measured from its own pixels: the largest region of thin ice cloud, how straight it is (from the principal axes of its pixels), its width, and how far the nearest advected flight track lies from it. A two-rule classifier then decides: straight means contrail, and a track within 10 km attributes it. There is no trained model yet; the planned one is a segmentation network trained on Google's labelled OpenContrails set (GOES-16). Streaky cirrus and contrails crossing cirrus are the hard cases, and the rule misses some of them.

Aircraft stay low. Rockets and re-entries reach layers nothing else does

Each row is a 5-km slice of atmosphere, from the ground to 120 km. Aviation burns vastly more fuel, but almost all of it below 15 km, where rain washes particles out within weeks. Rockets put soot, alumina and chlorine straight into the stratosphere and above, and re-entering spacecraft vaporise in the mesosphere. Pick one substance at a time: they can’t be added together.

Regulators · researchersThe trade-off

Built to burn up: safer on the ground, more metal in the sky

Rules now make satellites in low orbit come down quickly. In 2022 the US Federal Communications Commission required the satellites it licenses to leave low orbit within five years of finishing their mission, down from a 25-year guideline. Satellites are also designed to burn up completely on the way down, so no debris reaches the ground.

That is safer for people below, but it puts the most metal into the upper atmosphere. In 2023 NOAA research aircraft found aluminium and other metals from re-entering spacecraft in about 10% of the sulphuric acid particles they sampled in the stratosphere. What that metal does to the ozone layer and the climate is still being studied.

FCC Second Report and Order on orbital debris, IB Docket 18-313, 29 September 2022 (coverage). Murphy et al. (2023), Metals from spacecraft reentry in stratospheric aerosol particles, PNAS 120, e2313374120.

  • Everything on this page is simulated. The humidity field, flights, carriers, satellite detections and space events are generated to show how the tool behaves.
  • Contrail formation assumes the Schmidt–Appleman criterion is met at every level above FL300, which is usually but not always true.
  • Energy forcing per km is a rough stand-in for CoCiP output: strong at night, weaker or net-cooling in midday sun, scaled by humidity and aircraft type.
  • Flights are real; their contrails are modeled. Tracks and operators come from OpenSky, humidity and wind from ERA5, and contrail formation, lifetime and energy forcing from pycontrails CoCiP.
  • AGWPCO₂ over 100 years is taken as 1.48 × 10¹² J per tonne. Contrail efficacy is not applied.
  • Rocket and re-entry values are illustrative placeholders. Black carbon and alumina in the stratosphere are not yet well quantified, which is why their ranges span an order of magnitude.
  • Reroutes change altitude only across the humid stretch plus 100 km either side, using the semicircular flight-level rule.
